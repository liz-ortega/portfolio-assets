01 · context

government UI is overwhelming.

USPS serves millions of people every day, but renting a PO Box is buried under dense menus and jargon. Over a 15-day sprint for my graduate course at Claremont Graduate University, I researched why, and redesigned the path in.

My replica of usps.com: “Quick Tools” opens 15 equal-weight icons, with the PO Box option in the middle.
02 · the challenge The checkout flow works. Users just can't find it.

The problem wasn't the rental form itself. It was everything before it: the hide-and-seek navigation that people had to survive just to get there.

03 · the shift

from hide-and-seek to front door.

buried in multi-layered dropdowns → one clear front door, above the fold.

Fix discoverability first, because confidence is lost long before checkout begins.

04 · the study

five people, one simple task.

PARTICIPANTS5 new rentersPeople who had never rented a PO Box before.
METHODmoderated think-aloudRun on a self-built replica of the live USPS site, so every session had the same conditions.
TASKfind it, rent itLocate and rent a PO Box, starting from the homepage.
what we found
INSIGHT 0180% failure rateParticipants spent an average of 77 seconds just searching for the rental page.
INSIGHT 02sizing confusionTechnical box dimensions meant nothing without real-world context.
INSIGHT 03the trust gapHide-and-seek navigation destroyed confidence before checkout even began.
Only 20% found the rental flow on their own. Once there, 100% finished it. The problem was finding it.
The size picker: XS to L, explained only in inches.

The discoverability deficit: “Quick Tools” had no visual hierarchy, and menu labels like “Business” vs. “Personal Mail” sent people in the wrong direction.

05 · solution

make the front door easy to find.

Drag to compare the original “Quick Tools” menu with my version, sorted by task under the same kind of headings USPS already uses in its Receive and Business menus.

06 · the redesign

fixes, right where people got lost.

All desktop, since that's where I ran the study. The first two are my menu and homepage screens, refined to follow USPS's own patterns. The last two are my mockups from the final presentation, shown as I made them.
NAVIGATION · REFINEDQuick Tools, sorted by taskBefore: 15 icons with equal weight. P1: “Didn’t know I could click the icon.”After: three columns under headings, the same way USPS already labels its Receive and Business menus. Rent or Renew a PO Box sits in Manage Your Mail with a short helper line, and it’s also listed under Business, since P5 said “I thought rental would be under Business.”
HOMEPAGE · REFINEDvisible, without taking overBefore: renting a PO Box was only reachable through menus.After: a row of task cards under the hero. Rent or Renew a PO Box is one of six everyday tools, marked with a red accent so it’s easy to spot without becoming the whole page.
MY MOCKUP · FROM THE PRESENTATIONInteractive “Compare Sizes” toolSupports exploratory interaction through a responsive tool where users can toggle between sizes, see dimensional illustrations, and compare fit for different mail volumes, minimizing uncertainty and enhancing user confidence.Why: in the rental flow, sizes showed as photos and dimensions (like 3" × 5.5"). USPS does describe what fits, but only behind a small info icon, and P2 said “Choosing a size was confusing.” This puts that comparison in view.
MY MOCKUP · FROM THE PRESENTATIONSimplified, step-by-step workflowStreamlines the reservation process by presenting key information in one centralized, easy-to-scan layout. Content flows step-by-step from top to bottom, reducing overwhelm and guiding users through each action without distraction.

07 · decisions

I tested on a replica I built of the live USPS site. why not just test the real site? A replica kept conditions identical for every participant, so differences came from people, not from a changing website.

10 · reflection we found the front door nobody could find.

Control the canvas. My first big prototype taught me that things misalign if you don't control the layout. I learned the magic of frames, constraints and auto layout to replicate a real environment.

Moderation is hard. Staying neutral, giving clear instructions and watching micro-interactions all at once forced me to observe without leading. A tight script became my helper.

Visuals tell the real story. Usability metrics get lost in dense text, so I learned to show the data visually.

“If you want stakeholders to care, you have to show them the data.”
