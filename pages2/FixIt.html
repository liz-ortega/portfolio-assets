Fix It in Post-It, empathy you can play.

A pixel-art platformer where you play a sticky note crossing campus, living a day in three classmates' shoes, then leave each level with a “How might we…” question. I designed and built it to make the empathize stage of human-centered design hands-on for college students.

01 · context

design is a way of thinking, not a job title.

I believe design gives anyone, in any industry, a framework for working through the problems they run into. That's why I founded our design club, and why I chose to lead the Campus Design Lab: campus problems are ones everyone feels connected to, and students often have real ideas but don't know how to move them forward.

For the “Arcades Everywhere” project in my Interactive Media Studio class, I wanted to bring those two worlds together and make the first step of human-centered design something you could play.

why “fix it in post-it”? The name is a pun with two meanings.
MEANING 01 where ideas start Sticky notes are everywhere in our ideation sessions at The Hive, where our club meets and one of the creative hubs of the Claremont Colleges. They're how every idea begins, which is why the main character is a sticky note.
MEANING 02 a band-aid fix Institutions often “fix it in post”: patching problems after the fact instead of fixing the system. The game names that habit.
THE POINT from note to action Every level ends on a “How might we…” so the sticky note becomes a starting point for real, actionable change.
02 · the challenge Everyone agrees empathy matters. Few people practice it.

In intro design classes and club workshops, “empathize” is usually taught as a slide or a definition. Students nod along, then jump straight to solutions. Meanwhile, the everyday friction on campus (being sick with a full schedule, losing something, skipping lunch) is so normal that people just push through it and never question it.

There's a second gap too. Our Campus Design Lab knows a lot of campus problems, but the people who can change them, like administrators, don't always feel them. How do you start that conversation with someone who has never lived the problem?

03 · the shift

from reading about it to living it.

a definition of empathy on a slide → two minutes in someone else's day.

Instead of explaining empathy, the game makes you feel a classmate's friction through its mechanics, then hands you a “How might we…” so the feeling turns into a design question.

04 · research

frictions people ignore.

I started from my own experiences on campus and conversations with other students. I chose three frictions I personally resonated with and heard about again and again, especially the sick student, which everyone related to. While the Campus Design Lab gravitated toward clear-cut problems we could design for right away, I deliberately picked ones that have been problems for years and that nobody quite knows how to tackle. These three are a starting point for building the framework; next, I'll bring it back to our board and apply it to more problems.

FRICTION 01no flexibility when sickCampus systems assume you're well enough to show up in person.
FRICTION 02lost and never foundThere's no clear place to look, so people retrace a whole day from memory.
FRICTION 03no time to eatBack-to-back classes and long lines turn lunch into something students skip.
05 · the game

a campus walk, sketched on graph paper.

The whole world looks like doodles in a notebook, the same place most design ideas start. You walk left to right across familiar campus scenery using the arrow keys and space bar, meeting three characters along the way.

01 · NOTICEa “?” appearsWalk close to a character and a small prompt invites you in, without forcing it.
02 · LISTENread their storyEach character shares their frustration in their own words.
03 · LIVE IT“Live My Journey”One button drops you into a mini-level built around their friction.
04 · DEFINEHow might we…?Every level ends with an HMW, the bridge from empathy to the next design step.
first-time game designer · what i studied

learning from games that make you feel effort.

This was my first real game. Before building, I studied games that turn strain into play, and borrowed their feel for Levels 1 and 2.

INSPIRATION 01CelesteA climb that feels hard on purpose. It shaped the vertical, platform-by-platform ascent of the sick student's level.
INSPIRATION 02Flappy BirdConstant, intense tapping. I used that rhythm to make concentration and a tired body something you feel in your hands.
06 · levels

every mechanic is the problem.

I didn't want mini-games that were just fun. Each one is designed so the controls themselves recreate the frustration.

LEVEL 1 The Sick Student “There's zero structural flexibility when your body gives out.” The mechanic: A vertical climb: the camera follows you up platform after platform while a color-coded to-do list crosses off campus buildings, the sky fades from day to night, and “So tired…” bubbles pop up. What you feel: Reach the top and a brand-new list appears. The mechanic makes the feeling literal: you're never really done. How might we make campus systems flexible enough to support students when they're not at their best?
LEVEL 2 Lost Their Item “I lost my water bottle and can't remember where it may be.” The mechanic: A left-to-right walk past every landmark you visited that day, searching each one as night falls. What you feel: Building after building returns an ✕ while the sky goes dark, and the level ends on “Still not found.” There's no system to check, only guessing. How might we make campus spaces feel more connected, so no one has to search alone?
LEVEL 3 The Hungry First-Year “I have exactly 15 minutes between classes, and the line is wrapped around the building.” The mechanic: Exit the building, then jump over a line of people, dinosaur-game style, while a clock ticks from 12:30. What you feel: The people in line keep pushing you back while a one-minute timer runs. When you finally get food, it's 1:05 PM and you're late for class. How might we design campus routines around students' real needs, not just their schedules?
07 · decisions

You always get through a level, but you never win. The water bottle stays lost, the to-do list starts over, and you're late for class. Why no victory screen? That lingering disappointment is the point: it mirrors problems that don't get solved, then hands you a How Might We so the question becomes yours.

a framework, made playable.

It's built as an education tool, for two audiences. For our club (~40 members) and Intro to Human-Centered Design students (~20 per section), it shows there's more than one way to empathize: interviews matter, but so does stepping into someone's shoes.

For administrators and others who don't live these problems, it's a conversation starter. Playing it in public breaks a problem down in two minutes and ends on a How Might We, so the conversation starts from a shared question instead of a complaint.

The Problem Fair and Fix It in Post-It come from the same belief: frameworks like identifying and defining problems stick when they're fun, social and tied to things people actually live with. Leading the lab has taught me to design experiences for people learning design, which is its own kind of product.

10 · reflection we turned “empathize” into something you can feel.

Mechanics are a message. Designing the controls taught me that how something feels to use can say more than any text on screen.

Make frameworks approachable. Design thinking is useful in every field, but only if people can get into it. A game lowers the barrier.

Simple vs. repetitive. As a first-time game designer, the hardest part was deciding what to simplify and what to repeat so the feeling lands without explanation.

Context changes clarity. Level 1's call to action was instantly clear. Level 2 landed with 5C students, who know those buildings are spread across campuses, but needs more for outsiders. Next, I want to show how far apart “close” buildings really are.

Next: co-design the next version with the community so their ideas shape it, add a browser version, and build more games for other stages of design thinking, like defining and ideating, and for communities beyond campus: Claremont, California and other cultures.

“The problems we push through every day are the ones most worth designing for.”
